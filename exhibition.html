Antizionism

Antizionism demonizes the Jew through the Jewish nation-state. Like antijudaism and antisemitism before it, antizionism portrays its Jewish target, Israel, as the embodiment of evil. Today, that evil is defined through the language of human rights. Israel is therefore accused of the era’s worst moral crimes: racism, colonialism, apartheid, genocide, and Nazism.

Although antizionism emerged as a full-scale ideological campaign in the Soviet Union after 1967, its roots appeared much earlier. Antizionist ideas can already be found in Lenin’s writings in 1903 and later in the 1952 show trial of Rudolf Slánský, the Jewish General Secretary of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia, who was accused of participating in a “Zionist conspiracy” against the state and subsequently executed. Slogans such as “Zionism is racism” and “Israel is committing a genocide” come from the Soviet Union. Few know the history of antizionism.

Three Streams, One River: The Making of Antizionism

At the heart of the exhibition is Three Streams, One River: The Making of Antizionism, a historical narrative demonstrating how three distinct ideological streams converged to shape the modern antizionist movement:

The Arab world, where opposition to Jewish national self-determination became a central political and ideological movement.

Nazi ideology, whose propaganda and demonization of Jews continued to influence anti-Jewish narratives after the Second World War.

The Soviet Union, which transformed traditional antisemitic narratives into the language of anti-Zionism, anti-colonialism, and political activism, exporting these ideas throughout the world.

Soviet Antizionism

The history of Jew-hatred in Russia predates antizionism. The Russian Orthodox Church played a huge role in galvanizing the people to attack Jews in the Pale of Settlements. Jews were often referred to as zhidy, or kikes.

When the burgeoning Soviet state was forming, Lenin was against Zionism but not because he saw in it something inherently evil, but because he was against all forms of ethnic or religious nationalism. He saw in Zionism a form of Jewish nationalism.
